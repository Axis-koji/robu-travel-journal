Why Generative AI Can Create Team Friction—and Five Ways to Clarify Responsibilities

AI may reveal problems rather than create them

Hello, I'm Roboo.

A team adopts generative AI, expecting work to move faster. Instead, review work increases and disagreements become more visible. When that happens, blaming the technology alone may miss the real issue.

To delegate a task to AI, a team must put its process, decision criteria, and definition of done into words. That exercise can expose differences that were previously handled through unspoken assumptions. This does not describe every workplace in Japan. However, in teams where people often expect one another to “read the room,” AI adoption can reveal responsibilities that were never clearly assigned.

AI cannot reliably fill in vague instructions

Even within one department, people may approach the same task differently. One person may want to define the full plan first, while another prefers to create a small part and adjust as they go. Colleagues can sometimes bridge that gap through shared history and context. An AI prompt cannot do so unless the relevant expectations are stated explicitly.

Making the process explicit takes effort, but it also has value. It helps a team identify procedures and standards that everyone assumed were shared, even though they had never been discussed.

Invisible work can fall on a small number of people

In a survey published by Persol Research and Consulting in February 2026, people who used generative AI four or more days per week accounted for 11.7% of respondents. The survey also found an average time saving of 16.7% for tasks completed with generative AI, yet only 25.4% of users reported an overall reduction in their working hours.

The report recommends that organizations avoid leaving adoption to a handful of experts or a digital-transformation team. It suggests separating the role of experimenting with AI from the role of sharing practices across the organization. Without clear ownership, training, review, and troubleshooting can become unpaid or unrecognized work for a small group of helpful employees.

“Workslop” creates more rework

BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab use the term “workslop” for AI-generated work that looks polished but lacks the substance needed to move a task forward. In a September 2025 survey of 1,150 full-time U.S. desk workers, 40% said they had received workslop in the previous month. The researchers estimated that it accounted for 15.4% of the work respondents received.

These results come from the United States and should not be treated as a direct measure of Japanese workplaces. The underlying risk is still useful to consider: when an AI-generated draft is passed along without adequate review, the recipient may have to reconstruct the context, check the facts, and redo the work.

Define three levels of responsibility

Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry organize the AI Guidelines for Business, Version 1.2, around three types of parties: AI developers, AI providers, and AI business users. The guidelines also emphasize principles including transparency, accountability, and appropriate record-keeping.

An ordinary in-house team does not need to reproduce this formal structure. A practical starting point is to name three responsibilities:

  1. The person who instructs the AI and prepares the draft
  2. The person who checks facts, rights, and wording
  3. The person who makes the final decision to submit or publish the work

Five points to confirm before and after adoption

  • Which tasks will be delegated to AI, and where will human judgment begin?
  • Who will review AI output, and what standards will they use?
  • Whose working time will include review and correction?
  • Who should be consulted when the result is unexpected?
  • Where will the prompt, output, and reasons for revisions be recorded?

A team does not have to settle every question at once. It is often more practical to start with a small task, record where rework occurs, and adjust the responsibilities as the process develops.

Robu's Wrap-Up

When generative AI creates friction within a team, the technology may not have suddenly damaged the team. It may simply have made long-standing ambiguities in roles and expectations easier to see.

The answer is not to leave everything to the most experienced AI user. Define who drafts, who reviews, and who makes the final decision, and create a clear place for questions. With those foundations, AI adoption can become an opportunity to improve the way a team works rather than a source of conflict.
