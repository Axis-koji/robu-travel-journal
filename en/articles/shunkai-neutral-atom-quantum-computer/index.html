Arranging Atoms with Light to Compute: Quantum Computer Shunkai Needs No Large Cryogenic Cooling System, Starting at About 50 Qubits and Aiming for 10,000 Physical Qubits by FY2030

Use a single atom as the smallest unit of computation. Then arrange those atoms with light.

On August 24, 2026, the Institute for Molecular Science (IMS), part of the National Institutes of Natural Sciences, announced that "Shunkai" (春海) had begun operating: Japan's first full-stack neutral-atom quantum computer, and one of only a few in the world, according to the institute. Let me say up front that this is not a product you can buy; it is a research and development platform. I haven't visited or used it myself, and this article is based on the official announcement and related primary sources.

In Robu's Selection I usually pick specific products, but this time I chose, as something good from Japan, the foundation of a technology that Japanese research institutions and companies are growing.

What Kind of Machine Is Shunkai?

"Full-stack" here means a complete system that spans the software all the way to the computing hardware. At a press conference on August 25, 2026, Minister Onoda of the Cabinet Office also touched on the point that it was realized as a system covering everything from software to the computing device.

At this point, Shunkai is a research and development platform of about 50 qubits in its initial stage. External use is planned to be opened up partially in the future. It is at the stage of starting from this scale and growing from there.

How Neutral Atoms Compute: Arranging Atoms with Light

According to the Institute for Molecular Science, the neutral-atom approach works like this:

  1. A single atom is used as a qubit, the smallest unit of computation
  2. Optical tweezers trap the atoms in an array
  3. Microwaves and lasers are used to perform the computation
  4. A camera observes the fluorescence of the atoms to read out the result

"Arranging atoms with light" sounds almost unreal, but the steps themselves are surprisingly orderly: capture, arrange, compute, and read from how they glow. I feel that this clarity is one of the appeals of the approach.

The Official Strengths, and What Makes This Approach Interesting

The institute lists the following as characteristics of this approach:

  • The overall system operates at room temperature and does not need a cryocooler, the large dedicated cooling system that keeps the computing part of a quantum computer at extremely low temperatures
  • By moving atoms during computation, entanglement can be created between arbitrary qubits
  • The arrangement of atoms can be changed to suit the algorithm
  • The number of qubits can be increased relatively easily
  • Information can be retained for a long time

The atoms themselves are cooled to extremely low temperatures, while the overall system operates at room temperature. That means the computing section does not have to be kept inside a large cryogenic cooling system. Simpler cooling equipment, atoms whose arrangement can be changed, and qubits that are easier to add: to me, these three are what make this approach most interesting.

At the same time, the institute also names scaling up, computational errors, and long-duration stable operation at high fidelity as challenges for the future. Getting past them one by one seems to be the road ahead.

The Industry-Academia Team Behind "Japan's First"

"Japan's first" does not mean it was assembled from domestically made parts alone.

Shunkai is an industry-academia collaboration with IMS at its center, where the software stack was developed by Hitachi and the QPU stack was developed jointly with the U.S. company Infleqtion. The Cabinet Office press conference also described it as realized through cooperation among industry, academia, and government. Combining strengths from inside and outside the country to stand up a machine that actually runs in Japan: that is where I see the greatest value of this machine.

There are also primary sources for background. Hitachi has announced that it is participating in a research project on neutral-atom fault-tolerant quantum computers running from fiscal 2026 through fiscal 2030. The page for the Ohmori project under JST Moonshot Goal 6 lets you see the research plan and development structure aimed at running quantum computers that use atoms while detecting and correcting calculation errors.

From the Present to the Goals

Shunkai, now in operation, has about 50 qubits. The institute plans to expand to about 500 qubits after that. By fiscal 2030, the goal is to have 10,000 physical qubits together with error detection and correction functions that external users can use. It is a path of widening the scale in stages: about 50, about 500, then 10,000.

What the official materials and the press conference point to are external use, development of new applications, a demonstration of quantum error correction, and a concept for a quantum-GPU hybrid center combined with existing supercomputers. Researchers and companies start using it, applications emerge, error correction gets tested. Within that flow, I see a place that connects with existing supercomputers taking shape.

The Edo-Period Astronomer Behind the Name

The name "Shunkai" comes from Shibukawa Shunkai, an astronomer of the Edo period. He was the person who created Japan's first original calendar system.

IMS explains that the calculation of celestial motion evokes control on the Bloch sphere, which represents a quantum state, and that the machine was named with the hope that it would perform precise quantum calculations as Shibukawa Shunkai once did. The name carries the hope that the machine will live up to Shibukawa Shunkai's legacy of precise calculation.

Shunkai is a research and development platform where Japanese research institutions and companies have begun running a complete system, from software to computing hardware. Drawing on strengths from inside and outside the country, they have stood up a machine that actually runs in Japan. I'm looking forward to following the road ahead as it works through scaling up, computational errors, and long-duration stable operation, and moves from about 50 to about 500 qubits, and then to 10,000 physical qubits by fiscal 2030.

External use, new applications, a demonstration of quantum error correction, and a quantum-GPU hybrid center: I'm waiting to see what announcements come next.
