Are AI-Recommended Links Safe? Five Checks That Help You Avoid Scam Sites

Ask an AI assistant where to buy a product or how to contact a company and it may return a neat answer with clickable links. That is convenient—but it does not make every link safe.

Japan's National Police Agency warns that fake shopping and scam sites can appear high in search results. Google also says AI Overviews can make mistakes and advises people to check important information against multiple sources. An AI assistant can be a useful guide; it is not the guarantor of a destination's identity.

How can a bad link enter an AI answer?

This is not necessarily a story about an AI deliberately deceiving someone. Search and chat systems build answers from public web information, search results and external pages. If that information is deceptive or a legitimate site has been compromised, an inaccurate contact or unsafe link can enter the chain.

In February 2026, Microsoft described real-world attempts at what it calls “AI Recommendation Poisoning.” Some sites used pre-filled AI links containing hidden instructions intended to make assistants remember or favour a particular company. Microsoft reported more than 50 distinct prompts across 31 companies and 14 industries. It also stressed that effectiveness varies by platform and over time, while protections continue to evolve.

Anthropic's September 2026 threat intelligence report separately documented ways AI had been used in scams, fraud, cyber operations and influence activity. Neither report means every AI link is dangerous. The practical lesson is that attackers are learning to work with search and AI systems, so users must still verify sources and destinations.

A high search ranking is not proof of legitimacy

This problem predates generative AI. Japan's NPA describes cases where legitimate business servers were compromised and search visitors were redirected to fake shopping sites. It advises checking the domain, implausibly low prices, urgency language, company details and unnatural wording. It also warns that fake sites can appear in prominent search positions or social-media advertising.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission makes a similar point: the first result may be a paid ad, and a displayed phone number may belong to an impersonator. It recommends typing a known address directly or using a number from a bill or other trusted document.

Five checks before and after you click

1. Confirm the official domain yourself

Even when the organisation's name looks right, a slightly different domain can lead somewhere else. Reopen the site through a bookmark, official app, contract, statement or another route you already trust. Do not let the AI answer become your only doorway.

2. Cross-check prices, phone numbers and payment details

For purchases, transfers, account recovery, medical questions, legal matters or investments, compare at least two independent first-party sources. Fluent language does not guarantee that a number, contact or destination is correct.

3. Treat urgency as a warning sign

Stop when a page says “today only,” demands immediate payment, or tells you to call a single number at once. Extreme discounts and pressure tactics are among the warning signs highlighted by the NPA.

4. Be cautious about what you ask AI to analyse

Microsoft notes that every website, email or file supplied to an assistant can become an opportunity for injected instructions. Avoid untrusted “summarise with AI” buttons and links whose embedded prompt you cannot inspect. Check the sender and purpose before passing outside content into an AI tool.

5. Report suspicious results and restart from a trusted route

Google provides feedback channels for AI Overviews and reporting routes for phishing pages or suspicious redirects. For Japanese shopping sites, the NPA points users to SAGICHECK as an additional risk check. After reporting a page, do not reuse the same link—open the official address independently.

Asking AI is different from delegating trust

Using AI to discover options is reasonable. The risk rises when discovery, identity verification, payment and the submission of personal data all happen through one unverified chain.

A safer division of labour is simple: let AI help with discovery and comparison criteria, then let the user verify the official domain and important details through independent first-party routes. Before sending money or changing an account, step outside the AI conversation and check again.

Conclusion

An AI-recommended link can be a useful starting point, but it is not a safety certificate. Search position and polished prose do not prove that a destination is legitimate.

The rule worth remembering is: discover with AI, verify through official channels, and cross-check consequential actions through a separate route.
