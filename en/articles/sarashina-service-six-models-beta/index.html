Japan's Sarashina AI Is Free to Try: What Six Public Models Change

Hello, I'm Robu.

“A homegrown AI designed for Japanese” can sound interesting while still feeling distant. Product announcements and research posts may describe a model, but an ordinary user or a small development team often cannot see where to begin. On October 13, 2026, SB Intuitions opened a free beta of Sarashina Service, giving the public a new way to try its Sarashina family.

The release is broader than a single chatbot. It offers six models covering conversation, high-volume lightweight processing, text embedding, search-result reranking, safety classification, and Japanese-English translation. A browser-based chat provides a direct way to experiment, while APIs allow developers to test the components inside their own prototypes.

That does not make the beta a finished production service. The official announcement says that rate limits apply, the beta's end date is undecided, and usage data may be used to improve the models and service. The useful question is therefore not simply whether the service is free. It is what can be tested responsibly, with which data, and against which failure criteria.

The six models are different tools, not just six sizes

The public lineup separates several jobs that are often hidden behind one AI product:

  1. Sarashina3 mini is the standard model for text generation and conversation.
  2. Sarashina3 nano is a lightweight model intended for simple tasks at higher volume and speed.
  3. Sarashina3 embedding converts text into representations that can support semantic search across documents and FAQs.
  4. Sarashina3 rerank reorders retrieved documents by their relevance to a query.
  5. Sarashina3 guard classifies harmful or unsafe input and output text.
  6. Sarashina3 translate handles Japanese-to-English and English-to-Japanese translation.

RAG, or retrieval-augmented generation, means that an AI searches a set of documents first and uses what it finds as the basis for its answer.

Imagine an employee asking, “What is the deadline for applying for parental leave?” Embedding could search the internal rules for passages that may be relevant. Rerank could then put the closest matches first. Mini could write an answer grounded in the highest-ranked rules. Guard could check the question and answer for problems, while Translate could provide an English or Japanese version when needed.

In plain terms, the models divide the work into finding, selecting, answering, checking, and translating.

That workflow is not automatically reliable. The source rules may be out of date, an important document may be missing from the search collection, or the ranking criteria may not suit the task. Each of those conditions still needs to be tested.

Chat and API access serve different purposes

The web chat is the easier starting point for an individual. It can reveal how the model follows Japanese instructions, handles tone, and translates ordinary text. According to the announcement, users can sign in with a Google account and do not need to submit a separate application.

The APIs are for integrating individual functions into software. A developer could prototype FAQ retrieval, inquiry classification, translation, or a text-safety check. A disciplined evaluation should begin with public or synthetic data, not confidential customer records. The team should decide in advance what a correct answer looks like, which errors are unacceptable, how long a request may take, and what the application should do when a rate limit is reached.

This approach turns a casual demo into evidence. It also avoids the common mistake of judging a model from one impressive response while ignoring consistency and recovery behavior.

Japanese benchmarks are a starting point, not a production guarantee

SB Intuitions' technical material says that Mini and Nano were trained on more than 30 trillion tokens, primarily Japanese and English. Its official posts also describe seven task types for the embedding model and instruction-controlled reranking for different search needs.

The developer's public evaluation reports that Sarashina3 rerank performs at a level comparable with a leading Japanese reranker on the tested benchmarks. The same official post also explains important limits: practical evaluation tasks remain scarce, some public tasks depend heavily on Wikipedia-derived data, and small score differences may not reveal how well systems adapt to a real domain.

Those are healthy qualifications. The figures are developer-reported results, not universal proof that one model will outperform another in every company. A customer-support team should test its own abbreviations, product names, ambiguous questions, old documents, and cases where no answer exists. An embedding score is not the same thing as a useful answer.

Three conditions matter in a free beta

1. Usage data

The announcement says usage data may be used to improve the AI models and service. The terms of use and privacy policy control the details and should be reviewed before testing. Until an organisation has completed that review, confidential material, personal information, unpublished client documents, credentials, and regulated data should not be entered merely for convenience.

2. Rate limits and continuity

Requests are subject to a rate limit. The beta's end date is undecided. The announcement does not establish a permanent free tier, future pricing, a production service-level commitment, or guaranteed capacity. A prototype should therefore record its assumptions and include an exit path rather than silently becoming a critical production dependency.

3. Guardrails still need human governance

Sarashina3 guard can support harmful-text classification, but no classifier eliminates the need for policy decisions. Context, user age, industry obligations, false positives, false negatives, appeals, logging, and emergency shutdown procedures remain system-level responsibilities. A “safe” model response is not a substitute for a safe service design.

A practical order for testing

  1. Use only public information and test Japanese instruction-following in the chat.
  2. Check whether Translate preserves names, numbers, negation, formality, and domain terms.
  3. Build a small 20-to-50-item FAQ set and compare embedding-only retrieval with reranking.
  4. Save expected answers and failure examples, then compare Mini and Nano by task.
  5. Have a person review Guard decisions and record both false positives and missed cases.
  6. Review the terms, data handling, rate limits, and migration plan before expanding scope.

Robu’s Take

The most important part of this release is not the phrase “free Japanese AI.” It is the ability to examine generation, retrieval, ranking, safety, and translation through one public entry point. In a Japanese business workflow, a strong answer model cannot compensate for poor document retrieval, weak translation, or an undefined review process. The six-part lineup helps make the system nature of AI more visible.

“Homegrown” should not be used as a shortcut for assumptions about privacy, accuracy, or security. Those properties belong to a specific service, contract, deployment, and operating procedure. Users still need to ask what leaves their environment, how it may be used, what limits apply, and who acts when the system is wrong.

The best result of a free beta is not a spectacular screenshot. It is discovering where the system fails in your own use case while the test is still small and reversible. If teams begin with public data, preserve evidence, and keep people responsible for decisions, Sarashina Service can become a useful window into both the strengths and the boundaries of Japanese AI.
