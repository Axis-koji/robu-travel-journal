What Is Gemini Agent? From Research to Presentations, Explained Simply

Hello, this is Robu.

You ask AI to research a topic, read its answer, and then turn the information into a table or presentation yourself. AI helps, but you still do much of the work that follows. Does that sound familiar?

Google Cloud's announced Gemini agent is an enterprise offering intended to connect those steps. Rather than only answering questions, it aims to use tools and business systems to produce finished work. Google's announcement

Take preparing a meeting presentation

Research followed by a presentation is an easy example to picture.

Google describes a workflow that researches market trends, builds a financial model in Sheets and creates a presentation covering both. The idea is to connect tasks across applications.

If that reduces the copying and pasting between apps, it could be useful. For me, the attraction is less about getting a faster answer and more about reducing the work that comes after it. Google Cloud's overview

This is not simply the Gemini app you already use

The enterprise context matters here.

This announcement does not mean anyone can open the consumer Gemini app and immediately delegate the same tasks. It describes an environment connected to company documents and business systems, with controls over what the AI can access and do. Google's announcement

As an individual running a blog, my first questions are whether my subscription includes the relevant features and whether they connect to the services I use. Without those answers, I would not describe it as a new feature that will immediately help me.

Work that continues after you close your laptop

Google describes long-running cloud tasks whose progress and results can be checked later. Google Cloud's overview

But running for longer and producing a correct result are different things. Someone still needs to check the research and the figures in the finished document.

Three things behind the workflow

Skills: reusable ways of doing a job

Think of a skill as a work procedure rather than another piece of jargon. Google describes reusable instructions, knowledge and workflows that individuals or teams can share. Official explanation of skills and tools

For example, a monthly report may use the same source documents, metrics and structure each time. Defining that pattern could reduce the need to explain it every month. This is an illustrative example, not a workflow I have tested.

Reusing an outdated calculation could also repeat the same mistake. A saved procedure is not necessarily a correct procedure; someone needs to keep it up to date.

Dividing the work between agents

Google also describes creating task-specific agents to share the work, calling this multi-agent orchestration. Official explanation of agent coordination

For a presentation, imagine separate roles for gathering information, organizing figures and drafting the explanation. That division could help, but adding more agents does not guarantee accuracy. I would still want to check that their figures and assumptions agree in the final document.

Deciding what is allowed—and when to stop

For businesses, permissions and action records are important. Google describes controls over each agent's permissions, records of its actions, and project spending caps that pause the agent when triggered. Official explanation of permissions, auditing and cost controls

I would keep reading documents and preparing drafts separate from sending or publishing anything. I would start with gathering and organizing information, then read the result before it leaves my hands. Controls that let a business set those boundaries seem just as important as the automation itself.

What I would check before signing up

This article explains Google's announcement; it is not my hands-on review. Before considering it, I would check the features included in my contract, supported integrations, availability in Japan and costs.

Features that become useful through company-system integrations may not deliver the same benefit for an individual blog. I would test one task and count the time spent on setup and review, not just the number of documents produced.

Robu’s Take

Gemini agent is easier to understand as a shift from talking to AI toward delegating the work that follows that conversation.

What matters to me is not the length of the feature list, but how much effort it saves. If checking and correcting an AI-generated presentation takes longer than making it myself, it defeats the purpose.

What I want to see is how much real work I can reliably delegate—not just an impressive description.
