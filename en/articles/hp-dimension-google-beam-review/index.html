Should You Deploy HP Dimension with Google Beam? Seven Checks for a 3D Meeting System Starting at ¥6.6 Million

AI-generated editorial image; not an actual HP product photograph or Google Beam interface.

Google announced on October 5, 2026 that Google Beam, its AI-powered 3D video communication platform, had begun shipping to Japan.[1] In the Japanese market, HP offers the system as HP Dimension with Google Beam.

Although it resembles a large display, the objective is different from an ordinary video-conferencing screen. The system captures a remote participant and renders that person with visible depth on a 65-inch light-field display. It is designed to convey eye contact and body language without requiring 3D glasses or a headset.[2][3]

The wall-mounted model has a listed Japanese price of ¥6,598,900 including tax. A Google Beam license, installation, maintenance, room work, and some furniture are not included. Before treating it as a futuristic replacement for every meeting room, check the following seven points.

1. The core 3D experience is one-to-one

HP's product material states that 3D video is for one-to-one conversations. This is not a system that places an entire conventional group meeting into the same life-size 3D format.

Potential uses include executive meetings, important customer discussions, interviews, and expert reviews where subtle nonverbal signals matter. If most meetings are routine sessions with many participants, it may be difficult to justify the premium over a well-designed conventional room.

2. A 65-inch light-field display, with no glasses

HP describes a system that combines a 65-inch light-field display, cameras, spatial audio, and lighting that adapts to the room. Participants do not wear 3D glasses or headsets.

The goal is to make gaze, posture, and gestures easier to perceive. It is not a free-floating hologram. A more accurate description is a dedicated meeting-room installation that coordinates the display, capture system, audio, lighting, and network.

3. Google Meet and Zoom are central

HP Japan says the system supports native Google Meet or Zoom Rooms environments. It can provide one-to-one 3D conversations and conventional 2D group meetings, while the announcement also refers to interoperability with services such as Teams and Webex.[2]

Compatibility on a product page does not establish that every feature will work in an organization's current configuration. Buyers should test their actual meeting service, account structure, management tools, and the quality and controls available through interoperability.

4. Hardware starts at ¥6,598,900 in Japan

HP Japan lists the following suggested prices, including tax:

  • Wall-mounted model: ¥6,598,900[2]
  • Floor-stand model: ¥7,546,000[2]

The prices include a touch controller and two expansion microphones. They do not include the Google Beam license, installation and maintenance, a dedicated room, recommended furniture, or any required construction. A procurement comparison should therefore use total cost of ownership across locations, not the hardware price alone.

5. Network and power requirements matter

HP's product brief specifies at least 50 Mbps of symmetrical bandwidth to each unit at less than 100 ms latency. It lists power consumption of 600 W during an active call and 70 W when idle.[4]

Both ends of a two-site conversation need a reliable environment. A pilot should examine peak-hour latency, packet loss, firewalls, and failure handling—not only the headline bandwidth number.

6. Confirm fine specifications before ordering

HP's current Japanese product page refers to seven cameras, while HP Japan's August 2026 press release refers to six. A configuration update, a difference in description, or documentation timing could explain the discrepancy, but the public material does not establish the reason.

This article therefore does not treat a fixed camera count as a confirmed purchasing specification. The final model number, current specification sheet, installation configuration, and warranty coverage should be confirmed in writing with the seller. For an enterprise system at this price, the quotation and contract specification matter more than a marketing page.

7. Read outcome figures as vendor research

HP publishes Google Beam research results indicating improvements in recall, nonverbal behavior, and attention compared with conventional video calls. The figures are relevant to a purchasing hypothesis, but they are vendor-presented results and do not guarantee the same outcome in every organization.

A useful pilot would measure the number of high-value meetings, travel avoided, time to decision, utilization, and the cost of equipping the other location. The question is not whether the first demonstration feels impressive; it is whether the system improves measurable outcomes over the existing room.

Who may benefit—and who may not

A plausible fit

  • Organizations with frequent, high-value one-to-one meetings between fixed locations
  • Teams for which travel cost, travel time, and nonverbal communication are material
  • Buyers that can provide dedicated rooms, stable networks, and operational support
  • Organizations able to run a realistic pilot at both ends

A weaker fit

  • Teams whose meetings are mainly large recurring groups
  • Organizations unable to equip the other participant's site
  • Buyers that cannot budget for installation, support, rooms, and connectivity
  • Teams that have not yet improved the audio, lighting, and cameras in their existing 2D rooms

Robu's Take

HP Dimension with Google Beam is neither a consumer television nor a device that replaces every meeting. It is a specialized enterprise installation intended to make an important remote conversation between two people feel closer to an in-person encounter.

Beyond the ¥6,598,900 entry price, a buyer needs licenses, installation, support, a suitable room, a reliable network, and a compatible system at the other end. It is most credible where one-to-one communication quality directly affects business results and the organization can sustain regular use. The sensible next step is a two-site pilot that compares total cost and meeting outcomes with the existing alternative.
