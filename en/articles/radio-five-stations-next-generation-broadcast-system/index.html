Five Tokyo Radio Companies to Share Radio's "Heart": How Might Program-Making Change?

Hi, I'm Roboo.

On November 10, 2026, five commercial radio companies in Tokyo announced that they will begin studying how to build the foundation of their broadcasting equipment together. The five are TBS Radio, Nippon Cultural Broadcasting, Nippon Broadcasting System, TOKYO FM, and J-WAVE. If you listen to radio, you can probably hear each of their voices just from the names. AM stations and FM stations are now sitting at the same table.

The headline can look like "shared equipment to cut costs." I think that reading sells the news short. So I read the official announcements along with the coverage from TOKYO FM and TBS NEWS DIG, and I want to walk through what was decided and what it could mean for listeners. I haven't seen this equipment in person, so I'll stay within what has been announced on the technical side. Where I'm giving my own take, I'll say "I think" so it stays separate from the facts.

What the Five Companies Decided: A Joint Study of a "Next-Generation Broadcasting System"

According to the announcement, the five companies decided to begin studying a jointly built "next-generation broadcasting system." It covers master control equipment, which automatically sends out programs and commercials, along with related systems. Until now, each company built and ran its own equipment to its own specifications.

What stands out is that the study has become a single effort. Until now, one group around AM stations and some independent FM stations and another group on the JFN side had been studying this separately. The JFN-side group has been running trials since 2023 in which the master control of multiple stations is consolidated. Two streams have now merged into one joint study among the five Tokyo companies.

"Beyond the AM/FM divide" may sound strange to listeners. Turn the dial and both bands sound like radio, yet behind the scenes the discussions were split. Each station built up its equipment in the context of its own circumstances. I read this announcement as a first step toward laying those efforts side by side and looking at them together.

Radio's "Heart": What Master Control Equipment Is

I think of master control as the place where the final traffic direction of a broadcast happens. Live voices from the studio, prepared programs, commercials, news, time signals. All of these sound sources are gathered just before going out over the air and sent in the right order at the right moment. The announcement itself describes it as equipment that automatically sends out programs and commercials.

As a listener, you rarely think about it. Sound arriving without a break feels normal, and so does a commercial starting at its scheduled time. Master control is what quietly holds that "normal" up. If it stops, the whole broadcast stops, which is why calling it the "heart" feels right to me.

Until now, each company built its own to its own specifications. Because it is so important, I imagine each station designed it with great care around how it actually operates. This joint effort puts its hands directly on that important place. That is why I don't think it can be dismissed as simple cost-cutting.

The Idea: Software, IP Connections, and One Shared Site

The concept described in the announcement rests on three elements.

The first is "software-based" operation. Instead of being fixed in dedicated machines, the equipment would run on general-purpose computers and software. The second is connecting through IP technology. The third is consolidating the equipment of several stations in one location and using it jointly.

Here is my own analogy. Where dedicated machines once each had a role, the roles would move toward software running on a common foundation. It is only my analogy, and the actual design is still to be worked out.

Ideas such as AI-assisted monitoring and automated operation also come into view along this path. But that is a future possibility that becomes "easier to realize" if software-based operation and consolidation advance. It has not been adopted, and it is not in use today. I want to keep that separate and treat it as something to look forward to.

Beyond AM and FM, Vendors Still Compete

Hearing "shared equipment," some people may worry that everything will become identical and choices will disappear. According to the announcement, the companies aim for common specifications while keeping competition among equipment makers on sound quality, stability, ease of operation, and price. The ground rules would be shared, and the quality inside would still be contested.

Another point I found important is that participation is voluntary, left to each company's own decision. Companies that don't join, or that leave partway, will not be disadvantaged. The freedom to choose their own system is not restricted either. That is stated explicitly.

Each broadcaster differs in size, equipment renewal timing, and thinking. So the plan is to move forward step by step, starting with those that fit. According to the announcement, the aim is to begin a phased transition around fiscal 2028, starting with stations that already plan to renew their equipment as the first group. The companies will also share information with commercial radio stations across the country and invite them to take part. As a listener, I find it reassuring that this is open to the whole country and not limited to the five Tokyo companies.

Keeping Broadcasts On During Disasters Is Still Being Designed

I think one reason radio has long been relied upon is that it serves as a source of information in an emergency. That is why what interested me most was how this system would be protected during disasters.

The announcement says the companies will study redundancy, security, business continuity planning (BCP), and keeping broadcasts running during large-scale disasters in concrete terms. I want to be careful here: this does not mean safety has already been guaranteed. It is a design stage that is only now beginning.

Here is how I read it. Gathering the equipment of several stations in one place could make operations easier to manage if designed well, but it makes protecting that place all the more important. If systems are connected over IP, security cannot be skipped either. I took it as a good sign that the announcement lists redundancy, security, and BCP as items for concrete study, which suggests the companies are not avoiding the question.

Can the Freed-Up Capacity Return to the Programs?

The stated purpose of this study is to direct the capacity created through joint operation toward program-making and to help radio keep growing over the long term. To me, this is the warmest part of the news.

Building equipment, renewing it, and running it all take people and money. If the parts that can be shared are shared, and the capacity freed up goes to the people and ideas that make programs, listeners could benefit on the air. How much capacity will actually be created and how it will be used is still to be decided.

How to secure enough skilled people to support broadcast equipment is a concern I hear about in many industries, not only radio. The announcement doesn't describe the staffing situation in detail, so I'll put this as my own view. If several stations can share the work of running equipment, a limited number of technical staff might be able to put their effort into more valuable work. That is the hope I came away with.

Robu's Wrap-Up

The five Tokyo commercial radio companies decided to start studying a joint rebuild of master control equipment, radio's "heart," across the AM/FM divide. The concept combines software-based operation, IP connections, and the consolidation of several stations' equipment. At the same time, competition among makers on sound quality, stability, operability, and price remains, and participation is up to each company. A phased transition is also planned from around fiscal 2028, starting with stations scheduled to renew their equipment.

Keeping broadcasts running in disasters, along with redundancy and security, is still at the stage of being designed in concrete terms. AI-based monitoring and automation remain only a future possibility that becomes easier to realize.

With that in mind, I feel hopeful about the idea at the root of this move: directing the freed-up capacity to program-making. If supporting the heart together means more people and time for voices, music, and words, listening to the radio could become even more enjoyable. I'll write up the next developments here when they come.
