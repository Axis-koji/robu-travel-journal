Can Manga Make Travel Etiquette Easier to Notice? Reading JNTO's Baki Travel Tips

Hi, I'm Robu.

The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) and publisher Akita Shoten have announced “10 Smart Travel Tips for Japan,” a visitor guide that uses characters from the Baki manga series to introduce practical advice and etiquette.

The collaboration is designed to attract attention, but announcing an eye-catching campaign is not the same as proving that it changes behavior. This article separates what the official sources confirm from the real difficulties visitors reported in a Japan Tourism Agency survey.

What JNTO announced

According to JNTO's September 10, 2026 release, the ten topics were selected using feedback from JNTO offices in 26 cities around the world. The booklet is being produced in English, Korean, French and Spanish, together with posters and stickers. JNTO plans to distribute or display the materials at overseas travel events and major airports in Japan, and to publish a digital version online.

That international input matters. The list is not presented only as a set of rules Japan wants visitors to follow; it also reflects questions and uncertainties seen by offices that regularly work with prospective travelers.

What the ten tips cover

The official release highlights subjects including:

  • Hands-free travel through luggage storage or forwarding
  • Visiting regions beyond the largest cities
  • Behavior on public transportation
  • Taking your trash with you
  • Using licensed taxis
  • Etiquette at temples and shrines, when taking photographs, and at hot springs
  • The Japan Visitor Hotline as a source of help

This is broader than a manners checklist. It combines practical planning, movement, regional travel and emergency support with guidance about considerate behavior.

The problems visitors actually reported

The Japan Tourism Agency's FY2025 survey collected 4,110 responses from international visitors before departure. The leading difficulties were:

Issue Share of respondents
Too few trash cans 17.2%
Communicating with staff 15.4%
Crowding at destinations or in local areas 12.9%
Using transportation 11.3%
Too little or unclear multilingual signage 10.9%

At the same time, 43.7% said they had not encountered a problem. Many visitors therefore traveled smoothly, while the reported difficulties were spread across waste, language, crowding, transportation and signage.

Why manga may help—and what remains unknown

Long notices are easy to overlook during a busy trip. A familiar visual style or popular work may encourage some travelers to stop and read. Even people who do not know the series may notice a visually distinctive booklet more readily than a dense block of instructions.

However, the primary sources confirm the campaign's content, languages and planned distribution—not its results. They do not give a circulation figure, a measure of comprehension, evidence of behavior change, or detailed contractual information from Akita Shoten. It would be inaccurate to say the manga format has already solved any etiquette problem.

The shortage of trash cans, for example, can partly be managed when travelers carry their waste, but it is also an infrastructure issue. Crowding will not disappear through messaging alone. Clear communication and improvements on the ground need to support each other.

Practical help travelers can use

JNTO's Japan Visitor Hotline operates 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, in English, Chinese and Korean. It offers tourist information and assistance for accidents, illness and natural disasters. Saving the official page before a trip provides a practical fallback even without the booklet.

JNTO's responsible-travel guidance also explains luggage forwarding and storage, how to queue for public transportation, and considerate behavior on board. Advice is most useful when it gives travelers a realistic alternative rather than simply telling them what not to do.

Robu’s Take

“10 Smart Travel Tips for Japan” turns input from overseas offices into a four-language guide covering luggage, transportation, waste, religious sites, hot springs and emergency support. Manga is an interesting doorway into that information.

What we cannot yet say is whether it improved understanding or behavior. Published distribution figures, audience feedback and later evaluation would make that clearer. Japan needs both better ways to explain local practices and practical improvements that reduce friction for visitors.
