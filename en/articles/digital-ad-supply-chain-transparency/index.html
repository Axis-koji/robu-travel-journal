Who Sells a Blog's Ad Inventory in the AI Era? Lessons from a METI Seminar on Transparency

Hello, this is Roboo.

An advertisement may look like a single box on a webpage, but the transaction behind it can involve an advertiser, an agency, an advertising platform, one or more intermediaries, and the publisher. As AI-assisted bidding and automated operations expand, the process becomes faster while the path taken by the money can become harder to explain.

On September 28, 2026, Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry held an online seminar about transparency and publisher revenue in the AI-era digital-advertising supply chain. Using the official seminar description, related METI survey material, and IAB Tech Lab specifications, this article translates the issue into practical checks for a small publisher.

Automation does not remove accountability

METI's seminar description notes that AI agents and automated advertising operations may create new effects on revenue opportunities and business continuity for publishers. Automation can process enormous numbers of auctions quickly, but a complicated chain can make it difficult for a publisher to identify the seller of its inventory or understand the deductions made before revenue reaches the site.

The useful response is not to treat AI itself as the problem. Even when decisions are automated, someone must remain accountable for inventory authenticity, placement quality, partner management, and revenue reconciliation. Publishers still need records that people can review and explain.

Three risks created by an opaque chain

The official seminar page identifies counterfeit inventory, unauthorized resale, and ad fraud as structural concerns. Counterfeit inventory misrepresents an unrelated seller as an authorized source. Unauthorized resale makes the transaction path difficult for the publisher to track. Ad fraud directs spending toward invalid traffic rather than genuine readers.

These issues affect publishers as well as advertisers. Revenue that should reach a publication can leak through unclear routes, while low-quality or deceptive ads can damage the relationship between the publication and its audience.

What ads.txt actually does

The IAB Tech Lab's ads.txt specification gives publishers a public way to declare the companies authorized to sell their digital inventory. Buyers can compare that record with information in an ad transaction and avoid inventory sold through an unauthorized route.

Publishing an ads.txt file is not a one-time cure. Incorrect account identifiers, former partners left in the file, or missing ownership information can make the declaration unreliable. The current specification also supports owner and manager domains to improve the relationship between ads.txt and sellers.json.

METI's 2025 publisher survey reported that 63.3% of respondents knew about ads.txt, while 34.8% said they had implemented and published it. The figures indicate a gap between awareness and actual operation.

Five checks for a small publisher

  1. Confirm that the ads.txt file lists only current, authorized sellers.
  2. Match each publisher account identifier against the advertising dashboard.
  3. Check whether each relationship is correctly marked as direct or reseller.
  4. Record monthly revenue, impressions, and invalid-traffic changes.
  5. Remove expired relationships and investigate any route that cannot be explained.

Adding a date and the name of the reviewer to each audit makes later changes easier to trace. When revenue moves sharply, a publisher can then examine not only traffic but also seller relationships and invalid-traffic treatment.

Map the route before adding more advertising partners

Adding partners may create more selling opportunities, but it also creates more opportunities for duplicated settings and unexplained resale. A simple table containing the contract name, seller identifier, relationship type, contact point, and last review date can make the chain far easier to understand.

Roboo's conclusion

The more automated advertising becomes, the more important human-readable accountability becomes. A publisher should be able to say who is authorized to sell its inventory, which route a transaction followed, and how the resulting revenue was checked.

For a small blog, the first step is modest: review ads.txt against current contracts, document the result, and repeat the review. Trust depends not only on the advertisement readers can see, but also on the transaction path they cannot see.
