When an organization considers deploying robots, it is easy to begin with purchase price and capability. A mobile machine working around people also raises three less glamorous questions: who pays when the machine is damaged, what happens if it injures someone or damages property, and how the work continues while it is out of service.

Tokio Marine & Nichido announced insurance for autonomous mobile robots on October 26, 2026. The product is designed to bring those three concerns into one framework. This article summarizes only what can be established from the insurer’s announcement and public government and industry documents. I have not reviewed the policy wording, a quotation, or detailed underwriting conditions. The insurer says that terms and premiums will be set individually, so those details remain unknown here.

Which robots are covered, and when does it begin?

The announcement says coverage will be available from November 2026. It is intended for autonomous mobile robots equipped with physical AI and designed to handle multiple uses and environments, including wheeled, quadruped and humanoid types.

That description does not mean every machine called a robot automatically qualifies. It points to systems that move autonomously and use AI to respond to different environments. Their risk profile may differ from a fixed industrial machine repeating one task on a closed production line. That distinction is my interpretation, not a statement made explicitly by the insurer.

Tokio Marine & Nichido also says it will examine the use case and operating environment before setting the underwriting terms and premium. The same model could therefore be assessed differently in security, logistics or another setting. This does not appear to be a product that can be selected from a universal price list.

Three AI robot insurance coverages: preparing for damage, liability and downtime

The announcement identifies three areas of coverage.

First, it covers repair or replacement when the robot itself is damaged by an unforeseen and sudden event such as a natural disaster or collision.

Second, it addresses the deploying company’s legal liability for bodily injury or property damage. The announcement establishes the category, but it does not provide enough detail to decide how any particular incident would be handled.

Third, it covers business-continuity expenses after insured damage stops the robot. Examples include the cost of substitute personnel and rental of a replacement robot. For an organization using automation to relieve a labor shortage, downtime can recreate the very staffing gap the robot was meant to reduce.

Putting all three areas in one product may make it easier to discuss the complete risk picture internally. That is my assessment; the actual convenience and scope will depend on the final terms.

What is excluded, and what insurance cannot decide

One exclusion deserves special attention. The announcement states that loss caused directly or indirectly by a cyber incident is not covered.

AI robots depend on software and networks, so operational accidents and cyber events may not always look neatly separated in practice. A buyer should ask how that boundary is defined in the policy wording and whether separate cyber protection is needed. I cannot determine that line from the announcement alone.

Insurance also supports the financial aftermath of an accident; it does not create a safe operating environment. Product design, operating procedures and the allocation of responsibility among the manufacturer, operator and maintenance provider still need to be settled. Because the insurer will assess the use and environment, being able to explain those controls should be part of preparation. This last point is an inference, not a published underwriting promise.

Market expectations require careful reading

The announcement cites a government roadmap estimating that the global AI-robot market could reach about ¥60 trillion in 2040. It also refers to a goal of exceeding a 30% global share and capturing a ¥20 trillion market. These figures are an outlook and a policy target, not a guaranteed forecast.

The insurer also points to expected expansion in security, cleaning, logistics, construction, care and customer service, along with the potential to offset labor shortages. Many of these uses place machines close to people and property, which makes third-party liability an increasingly important part of deployment planning.

Robu's Wrap-Up

The most important change is that a robot decision can no longer stop at “Can we buy it?” It now needs to include “Can we plan for damage, harm to others and downtime?”

Before contacting the insurer, confirm whether the intended robot fits the autonomous physical-AI scope, describe its use and operating environment, and decide whether a separate response to cyber incidents is necessary. The existence of insurance may lower one barrier to adoption, but individually set terms and premiums mean that it cannot be treated as blanket reassurance. The policy wording and underwriting conditions must still be reviewed.

Insurance is not a substitute for safety. Even so, planning for the consequences of failure is one sign that robots are moving from experiments into routine work.
