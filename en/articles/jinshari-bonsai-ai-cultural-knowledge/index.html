Can AI Explain the Beauty of Bonsai? Inside JinShari's Attempt to Turn Tacit Knowledge into Words

AI-generated editorial image; not an actual JinShari interface or research material.

The team behind the bonsai management app JinShari is conducting a project to articulate bonsai aesthetics and eventually implement the findings in AI. The team demonstrated its work at the 10th World Bonsai Convention in Malaysia in August 2026 and launched an academic crowdfunding campaign in September.[2][3]

What makes the project notable is that its purpose is not simply to let AI score a tree. The published research plan asks when, by whom, and in what context expressions such as negative space, subtraction, and wabi-sabi became associated with bonsai. The team then intends to bring those findings back into an app used during everyday care.

There is an important distinction, however, between the vision on the official website, functions currently listed for the downloadable app, and AI features that remain prototypes or future plans.

What JinShari is trying to build

The official site describes two pillars: cultivation management and design suggestions.[1] The cultivation side is intended to organize records such as watering and fertilization and provide seasonal guidance suited to each tree. The design side aims to evaluate a user's bonsai in words, explain possible improvements, and suggest an idealized future form.

That addresses two different problems. First, correct care varies by species, location, season, pot, and the condition of the individual tree. Beginners can easily struggle to decide what to do. Second, the qualities that experienced practitioners notice are often tacit and difficult to explain across languages and cultural settings.

At the World Bonsai Convention, the team demonstrated an AI prototype that used a smartphone photo to visualize points of interest.[3] According to the team's report, the event also exposed a shared challenge: standards for evaluating tree form differ by country and cultural region. The project therefore argues for explaining the background of an evaluation instead of treating one Japanese form as a universal answer.

Reading roughly 300 specialist magazines before asking AI

The research plan published on academist has three phases:[2]

  1. Analyze roughly 300 bonsai magazines from the early twentieth century onward, using text mining for written discourse and image segmentation to quantify features of tree form.
  2. Compare bonsai with tea ceremony, ikebana, Japanese gardens, painting, literature, haiku, and Noh to identify shared ideas and distinct concepts.
  3. Turn the findings into a cultural database and implement them in JinShari so users can encounter cultural context while caring for their trees.

The sequence matters. Instead of prompting a general-purpose model to freely answer “What is bonsai beauty?”, the plan starts with primary material and investigates how the language developed. AI is meant to become an interface to researched cultural knowledge, rather than the source that invents that knowledge.

What is available now—and what is not

The Japanese App Store listing describes JinShari as a free iPhone app.[4] It can organize photos for each tree, work histories, pesticide-use records, and details such as species and form. The listing also says the interface supports Japanese and English.

The same listing marks AI chat consultation as a future feature. Its version history also says design evaluation and sample-image generation were disabled while newer versions are developed.[4] AI cultivation guidance, AI form planning, and pest or disease image diagnosis described in project materials include functions identified as prototypes, under development or being validated.[3][5] As of October 7, 2026, readers should not assume that every AI capability shown in the project's vision is generally available in the shipping app. The roughly 300-magazine analysis is also a research plan running from September 2026 toward app implementation in September 2028, not a completed result.[2][4]

Anyone testing JinShari should separate its current record-keeping functions from planned features and check the latest App Store description and version history.

Three cautions when AI explains culture

1. Do not compress culture into one answer

Bonsai evaluation can vary by species, school, region, and period. A phrase that appears frequently in the source material is not automatically the only legitimate interpretation. A useful system should distinguish common explanations from alternative viewpoints.

2. Separate cultivation advice from aesthetic suggestions

Watering, pesticides, and repotting affect the health of a living tree. Their risk is different from that of proposing a visual style. High-impact cultivation advice needs context about species, climate, pot, season, and the actual condition of the tree, as well as a route to a qualified human expert.

3. Return the user's attention to the tree

Good assistance should encourage observation, not obedience. Instead of merely saying “cut this branch,” an educational system could identify the material or aesthetic principle behind a suggestion and show other valid options. That would make AI a learning aid rather than an automated judge.

Why the project matters beyond bonsai

Digitizing culture sometimes stops at preserving photographs and text. JinShari's plan is to return that material to the moment of practice: watering, observing branches, and deciding what to do next. In that moment, a user could encounter the history and ideas behind the tree.

The same pattern applies to brewing, craft, musical performance, and cooking. When AI is used in a field where expert judgment cannot be reduced to a few measurements, the first requirement is not a spectacular generated result. It is a careful record of who noticed what, why they made a decision, and how that judgment changed over time.

Robu's Take

JinShari is both a bonsai record-keeping app and a research project that aims to articulate bonsai aesthetics through historical evidence. Its proposed sequence—approximately 300 specialist magazines, comparison with neighboring cultural fields, and implementation in an app—is an attempt to use AI without handing culture over to AI.

The current app's available record functions must be separated from AI functions that are under development or planned. The most useful measure of success will not be whether the model declares one tree form “correct.” It will be whether the system shows evidence and alternative perspectives, helping people observe their trees more carefully.
